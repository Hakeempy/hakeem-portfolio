## The problem

Six problems, each taken from a raw CSV file to a model that can be checked and used: heart disease,
breast cancer, bank loan approval, insurance cost, customer segmentation and movie recommendations.
Between them they cover the main kinds of machine learning work: classification, regression,
clustering and recommendation.

## How it works

Every notebook follows the same path: load and inspect, clean, explore, prepare features, train,
evaluate, then predict for a new example.

1. **Heart disease.** 302 patients after removing duplicates. Six classifiers are compared on the same
   split, and a random forest does best at 85% test accuracy. It is saved with Joblib.
2. **Breast cancer.** 569 tumours with 30 measurements each. Logistic regression scores 92% on the test
   set and sits behind a small function that labels a new sample benign or malignant.
3. **Bank personal loan.** 5,000 customers, only 480 of whom took the loan. ZIP codes are mapped to
   counties and then to four California regions, and the larger class is undersampled before training
   logistic regression, which reaches 91% accuracy on a balanced test set.
4. **Insurance cost.** 1,338 policies and four regression models. Gradient boosting scores best, with an
   R² of 0.88, and powers the [insurance cost app](/projects/insurance-cost-app).
5. **Customer segmentation.** 200 mall customers clustered with K-means on annual income and spending
   score. The elbow method points to five clusters, each described for a marketing team.
6. **Movie recommendations.** 4,803 films. Genres, keywords, tagline, cast and director become TF-IDF
   vectors compared with cosine similarity, and `difflib` matches a misspelt title to a real one.

## Decisions worth explaining

- **Compare before choosing.** The heart disease and insurance notebooks try several kinds of model
  instead of trusting the first. On insurance, support vector regression on unscaled features scored an
  R² below zero, while the two tree ensembles scored 0.86 and 0.88.
- **Balance the classes before trusting accuracy.** Fewer than one customer in ten took a loan, so a
  model that always said no would be 90% accurate. Undersampling makes 91% mean something.
- **Fix the data rather than drop it.** The negative years of experience all belonged to customers aged
  23 to 29, so I treated the minus sign as a typo and kept the rows.
- **Prepare only what needs it.** In the heart disease notebook, the linear and distance-based models get
  one-hot encoding and scaling, while the tree models train on the raw features.

## What I would change next

Each model is judged on a single train and test split. I would use cross-validation, report precision
and recall for the medical models, where a missed diagnosis costs far more than a false alarm, and put
the preprocessing and the model into one scikit-learn pipeline so exactly the same steps run at
prediction time.
